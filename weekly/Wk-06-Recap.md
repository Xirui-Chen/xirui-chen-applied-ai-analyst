# Week 6 Recap: Decision Evaluation Harness and Reliability Analysis

This week I focused on building a decision-model evaluation harness that could be used across several InGen product contexts. The main goal was to create something structured enough to support repeatable testing, but still simple enough that another engineer could understand and extend it without reading through all of the scoring code.

I started by designing a 50-scenario YAML test bank. The scenarios were divided across four product areas: 15 for Aido Rover, 15 for Sentinel Prime AI, 10 for Senpai, and 10 for Fari. The Rover scenarios focused on patrol-route selection under battery, safety, and priority constraints. The Sentinel scenarios focused on alert escalation, uncertainty, and bandwidth limitations. The Senpai and Fari scenarios covered more conversational decisions, including tutoring support, safeguarding, medication reminders, privacy requests, caregiver escalation, and emergency response.

For each scenario, I defined structured inputs, a list of allowed actions, one target action, and a short rationale explaining the expected behavior. I also added a shared schema, an action taxonomy, and a validator so that future scenarios can be added through YAML without changing the implementation. This was one of the most useful parts of the project because it forced me to separate the evaluation contract from the controller logic.

I then implemented a transparent rule-based baseline. The controller achieved 86 percent exact-match accuracy across the 50 scenarios. Performance was strongest on Senpai, where it matched all 10 target actions, and weaker on Rover and Fari, where several boundary cases required more nuanced tradeoffs. The seven mismatches were still useful because they showed where simple rules were too rigid, especially around resident autonomy, low-confidence health readings, energy infeasibility, operator handoff, and bandwidth-aware escalation.

The second scoring layer used google/flan-t5-base as a small open Hugging Face judge. Each scenario was scored across three seeded runs using seeds 7, 17, and 27, producing 150 judgments in total. I used a strict single-label output format and rejected free-form or multi-label responses. The final run had a 100 percent parse rate, no retries, and no invalid outputs.

The overall nominal Krippendorff's alpha was 0.725, with 78 percent exact agreement across the three runs. This suggests that the judge was reasonably consistent, but still better suited for exploratory evaluation with human review than for fully automated scoring. Reliability also varied a lot by domain. Aido Rover had the strongest agreement with an alpha of 0.905, followed by Sentinel Prime at 0.748. Fari was more limited at 0.627, and Senpai was the least stable at 0.366. The pattern made sense to me because the Rover cases were mostly based on structured operational constraints, while the Senpai cases depended more on conversational context, emotion, and support decisions.

One challenge this week was getting the judge output format to behave consistently. My first version allowed free-form responses, which created parsing problems when the model repeated the rubric or returned more than one label. I fixed this by changing the prompt to a strict classification format and using a stricter parser. Another challenge was that high agreement did not always mean the judge was correct. The model never gave a PASS to a non-exact action, but it also rejected many actions that exactly matched the target label. This showed that the model was conservative and sometimes paid too much attention to differences in rationale wording.

I also found that the PARTIAL category was underused. Out of 150 judgments, only five were labeled PARTIAL. This suggests that the model did not consistently distinguish between an action that was safe but suboptimal and one that was clearly wrong. That is an important limitation of the current rubric and something I would address in a future version by adding more balanced non-exact examples and human-reviewed labels.

By the end of the week, I completed the full harness, README, schema, action taxonomy, validation scripts, rule baseline, seeded LLM evaluation, reliability analysis, error analysis, and methodology note. The main takeaway for me was that a useful evaluation harness needs more than a model and a score. The test contract, parsing rules, failure analysis, and reliability checks matter just as much. The final result is not a production decision system, but it provides a clear and reproducible framework for testing decision behavior and identifying where human review is still necessary.
