# Week 8 Retrospective

Looking back at the eight weeks, the most surprising finding for me was how often the simpler or more structured approach ended up being more useful than the more sophisticated model.

I did not expect that at the beginning. When I started the anomaly-detection work, I assumed the LSTM Autoencoder would probably have an advantage on faults with a temporal pattern, especially accelerated battery degradation. That assumption turned out to be wrong in the actual benchmark. LOF and One-Class SVM were much stronger overall, and the LSTM performed especially poorly on the battery fault. The Week 8 follow-up made that even clearer: lowering the threshold and training for more epochs did not really solve the problem. What was interesting was that some battery-specific reconstruction channels did contain useful signal, so the model was not completely failing to learn anything. The problem was closer to how the signal was represented and combined.

That changed the way I thought about the rest of the project. I became less interested in asking which model was “best” and more interested in asking what evidence would actually justify using a model in a product. The same thing happened in the RL work. SAC looked much stronger than PPO on BipedalWalker, but it was also much more expensive to train. In the multi-agent experiment, the learned policy improved some metrics while making collisions worse. The decision-evaluation work had a similar lesson: a judge can be fairly repeatable and still not be reliable enough to replace an explicit target contract. I think this was probably the most important shift in my thinking during the internship.

The weakest part of my work is probably the reinforcement-learning section. I learned a lot from it, and I think the benchmark was useful for understanding stability, runtime, and coordination tradeoffs, but the environments were still far away from the actual InGen products. BipedalWalker, LunarLander, and Simple Spread are good controlled environments, but they do not capture the sensor noise, battery constraints, safety rules, communication delays, hardware variation, or task hierarchy that would matter for Aido Rover or Aido Humanoid. Because of that, I would be uncomfortable making any strong product recommendation from the RL results alone. I think the section works best as a set of transferable lessons rather than as evidence about which policy should actually be deployed.

There are also parts of the decision-evaluation work that I would want to strengthen. The scenario bank was useful, but the target actions were analyst-defined rather than reviewed by domain experts. That matters much more for Fari and Senpai than for a structured Rover route decision. The low Senpai judge reliability made this pretty visible. I do not think the right next step is just to use a bigger LLM judge. I would rather improve the ground-truth process first.

If this were a 12-week project instead of an eight-week project, I would spend most of the extra time making the benchmarks more product-shaped rather than adding more model families.

For telemetry and anomaly detection, I would extend the Rover dataset from one-day samples to multi-day or multi-week histories and include charge cycles, software-version changes, route differences, weather variation, and more realistic fault onset. Battery degradation especially needs a much longer horizon than the current experiment.

For RL, I would build one small Rover-like environment with energy constraints, route goals, localization uncertainty, and safety penalties. I think even one environment like that would be more valuable than adding several additional standard Gym benchmarks.

For decision evaluation, I would have a human review process for at least part of the scenario bank. For Fari and Senpai, I would want caregiver, education, or safety-oriented feedback on what the target behavior should be before evaluating a model against it.

I would also spend more time connecting the dashboard to the evaluation workflow. Right now the dashboard is a good analytical interface, but it is still mostly presenting saved outputs. A stronger version would allow someone to acknowledge an alert, flag a bad model decision, record why it was wrong, and feed that information back into the evaluation set.The biggest thing I would keep from this internship is the idea that physical AI is not just a modeling problem. By the end, I cared much more about the path from data to model to evaluation to human decision than I did at the beginning. I think that shift is probably the most useful thing I learned from the project.
